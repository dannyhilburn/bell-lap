How this works

Nothing here is magic and none of it is hidden. If you disagree with a number, you should be able to find out exactly where it came from and argue with it.

1. Turning one race into all your paces

Everything starts with VDOT — a single fitness number from Jack Daniels' Running Formula. It comes from two equations. The first says how much oxygen it costs to run at a given speed:

VO₂ = −4.60 + 0.182258·v + 0.000104·v²        (v in metres/min)

The second says what fraction of your maximum you can hold for a given duration — you can hold a bigger fraction for 5 minutes than for 30:

%max = 0.8 + 0.1894·e^(−0.01278·t) + 0.2990·e^(−0.19326·t)   (t in minutes)

Divide one by the other and you get VDOT. Run it backward and you get equivalent times at every other distance. This engine was checked against the published tables: VDOT 50 gives a 5K of 19:56 against 19:57 in the book, threshold 6:51/mi, and 400m reps at 1:27 — all matching.

Your zones are then percentages of VDOT:

  • E — Easy (70%) — 70 to 80% of your weekly mileage lives here
  • Sub-T (85.5%) — LT1, roughly 2.0–2.5 mmol/L lactate
  • T — Threshold (88%) — about what you could race for an hour
  • CV — Critical Velocity (92.5%) — between threshold and interval
  • I — Interval (99%) — around 3K–5K pace, reps of 3–5 minutes
  • R — Repetition (107%) — mile pace, for speed and running economy

2. Why the 800 gets its own math

Daniels never meant VDOT for the 800 and said so — it's roughly a third anaerobic, and aerobic math can't see that. Two runners with identical VDOT can be ten seconds apart over 800m depending purely on how fast they are flat out.

So this uses your sprint time to work out your anaerobic speed reserve — the gap between the fastest you can run and the fastest you can run aerobically:

ASR   = v_max − v_VO₂max
v800  ≈ v_VO₂max + 0.34 × ASR

Two athletes, both VDOT 50: one with a 26.0s 200 projects to 2:26, one with a 30.0s 200 projects to 2:36. Plain VDOT says 2:43 for both, which is wrong for both.

Honest caveat: that 0.34 is taken from the research but hasn't been tested on enough real runners to be trusted precisely. Treat the 800 number as a ballpark, not a promise.

3. Why easy pace is set by heart rate

This is the single most useful thing in here. The pace math will hand you an "easy" pace that's genuinely too fast to be easy — Daniels' easy zone is a wide band (59–74% of max) and the single derived number sits near the quick end of it. Run that every day and your easy days quietly become medium days, which is the most common way to train hard and improve slowly.

So heart rate sets the ceiling. Your cap comes from two anchors, and the more conservative one wins:

cap = min( 0.78 × LTHR , 0.715 × HRmax )

Then pace is worked out from oxygen cost, not from a heart-rate ratio. The relationship %HRR = %VO₂R is the one HR/oxygen link with real validation behind it, and the curve is bent so it passes exactly through your measured threshold rather than an average person's:

%HRR(h)  = (h − HRrest) / (HRmax − HRrest)
r        = %HRR(h) / %HRR(LTHR)
VO₂(h)   = VO₂rest + r × %VO₂R(threshold) × (VDOT − VO₂rest)
pace(h)  = solve VO₂ equation for v

This is why resting HR matters. Same runner, same threshold, different resting HR:

resting 40 → easy 8:05 – 9:13/mi
resting 48 → easy 8:13 – 9:30/mi
resting 56 → easy 8:24 – 9:50/mi
resting 64 → easy 8:36 – 10:16/mi

A 31 second per mile spread between runners a simpler model would hand identical paces.

The result gets checked back against Daniels' 59–74% easy range. If your cap lands outside it, the app tells you rather than quietly using a bad number.

Heart rate governs easy and long runs only. On reps under about two minutes HR lags too far behind effort to be useful — chase it there and you'll start too fast and finish too slow. Quality sessions go by pace.

4. How the season is shaped

The plan is built backward from your championship meet, not forward from today. Taper gets protected first, then the event-specific block, then the support block, and base training absorbs whatever's left:

taper = 1 week if season ≤ 6 weeks, else 2
spec  = 35% of what remains  (1–6 weeks)
sup   = 30% of what remains  (0–6 weeks)
base  = everything left over

Mileage starts exactly where you are now and climbs 8%/week in base, 6%/week in support, dropping to about 78% every fourth week to let you absorb it. Once the specific phase starts, mileage holds rather than keeps climbing — intensity carries the load from there, not more miles. It never crosses the ceiling you set, and if it reaches that ceiling early it backs off about 7% after two weeks rather than parking there — the number you enter is a limit, not a level to live at.

Your meets get handled by priority:

  • C — train through. The race replaces that week's workout. It doesn't get added on top.
  • B — small taper. Two workouts come out, mileage stays.
  • A — championship. Full taper, about 55% of peak mileage, one sharpener, rest day before.

5. Why the plan changes every week

A plan that repeats the same session for six weeks isn't a plan, it's a calendar. Two separate things move:

Your projected fitness rises. VDOT follows an S-curve that peaks at your championship — slow at first because adaptation lags training, steepest mid-season, flattening into the taper:

gain = 2.5 × trainingAge × √(weeks/12) × √(peak/40) × ceiling
V(i) = V₀ + gain × ½(1 − cos(π · i/(n−1)))

Newer runners improve faster, longer seasons yield more, higher mileage yields more, and it gets harder the fitter you already are. Every pace and goal time in the plan is recalculated from that week's number.

The workouts themselves grow. Within each phase: one rep added every second week (capped at +40%), rest shrinking 6% per step (floored at 55% of where it started), and pace tightening on its own as fitness rises. Down weeks and taper weeks step backward on purpose. Every set tells you what changed and why.

wk 9  5×1000m @ 3:28  rest 180s  [baseline]
wk10  5×1000m @ 3:28  rest 169s  [−11s rest]
wk11  6×1000m @ 3:27  rest 158s  [+1 rep, −22s rest]
wk12  4×1000m @ 3:26  rest 180s  [down week]

6. About goal times

Goal times come from your projected fitness that week, and they're never set slower than a PR you've actually run. If your projection hasn't caught up to your PR yet, the plan holds at your PR until it does.

Understand what these are: projections, not promises. They're built from population averages about how much runners improve, and you're one person, not a population. If you beat them, the model was conservative. If you miss them, the model was optimistic — that is information about the model, not a verdict on you. Put your real results back in and everything recalculates.

7. What this doesn't know

  • Heat and humidity. Not modelled yet. On a hot day your HR cap will slow you down more than the pace chart suggests — that's the cap doing its job, so trust it over the pace.
  • Sleep, stress, school, illness. None of it. A plan is a hypothesis, not an instruction.
  • Your actual results. Nothing you run feeds back in automatically yet. Update your inputs when you race.
  • Whether today is a good day to push. Only you know that. The abort criteria on each workout exist for a reason — when reps fall more than 2–3% off target, the session is done. Finishing a workout that's already fallen apart trains the wrong thing.

8. Where the training models come from

  • Daniels VDOT — Jack Daniels, Daniels' Running Formula
  • Norwegian Singles & Double Threshold — Marius Bakken's lactate-threshold model, built over two decades and 5,500+ lactate tests, and the training the Ingebrigtsen brothers are known for
  • Lydiard Progression — Arthur Lydiard's sequential blocks: conditioning, hills, anaerobic, coordination
  • Tinman CV — Tom Schwartz's critical-velocity approach

Built as a prototype. The math is checked and the reasoning is above — but it's a tool, not a coach, and it doesn't know you. Use it with someone who does.
